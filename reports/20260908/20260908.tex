\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

Later stages of the visual cortex are thought to contain neurons
selective for progressively higher-level visual features. One
experimental approach to detecting regional differences in visual
processing is through presenting visual texture stimuli (e.g., sand,
tree bark, granite, etc.). Families of visual textures can be
parameterized by \(N\)th order joint pixel densities, so the
decodability of texture families in neural responses can indicate
selectivity for image statistics. Previously, Ziemba et al.~(2016) found
better within-family separation of neural responses in V1 and better
between-family separation in V2
{[}\citeproc{ref-ziemba_selectivity_2016}{1}{]}, and related work found
that texture stimuli with matched higher order image statistics produced
higher firing in V2 and V4
{[}\citeproc{ref-freeman_functional_2013}{2}--\citeproc{ref-lee_emergence_2025}{4}{]}.
However, existing analyses lack comparisons with IT, which responds more
sparsely to stimuli. Neural responses in IT are sparse in two different
ways: first, most IT neurons will not respond to any texture stimuli;
and second, of the IT neurons with any response to texture, only a small
number of unique stimuli will produce a response. The between-family
separability in IT compared to V4 can change depending on the handling
of reliable and responsive sites, suggesting that IT efficiently encodes
higher-level image statistics.

\section{Extended abstract
(tentative)}\label{extended-abstract-tentative}

\subsection{Higher- and lower-order visual areas may be characterized by
differences in processing image
statistics}\label{higher--and-lower-order-visual-areas-may-be-characterized-by-differences-in-processing-image-statistics}

Later stages of the visual cortex are thought to contain neurons
selective for progressively higher-level visual features. The primary
visual cortex (V1), for instance, contains neurons that selectively
respond to simple image features like oriented edges. In contrast, the
inferior temporal gyrus (IT), which handles later visual processing,
contains areas selective for object categories, such as the fusiform
face area for facial recognition.

One approach to characterize the relevant visual features for each layer
is through recording neural responses to visual texture stimuli, which
are approximately homogeneous images with repeated elements, e.g.,
images of sand, tree bark, granite, or honeycomb. Texture categories
(families) can be parameterized by a set of image statistics, where
image statistics are \(N\)th-order empirical densities of pixels.

Unique texture samples within the same family share image statistics and
should elicit comparable responses in the visual area sensitive to those
particular statistics. Patterns in selectivity for higher- and
lower-order image statistics can provide evidence for how visual
information is propagated or summarized in visual areas.

\subsection{Existing results find a tradeoff between selectivity for
higher- and lower-order
statistics}\label{existing-results-find-a-tradeoff-between-selectivity-for-higher--and-lower-order-statistics}

Previously, Ziemba et al.~(2016) found better within-family separation
of neural responses in V1 and better between-family separation in V2
{[}\citeproc{ref-ziemba_selectivity_2016}{1}{]}. Relatedly, Okazawa et
al.~(2015, 2017) compared the selectivity of V2 and V4 neurons for image
statistics in texture stimuli and found V4 neurons tended to be
selective for higher order statistics
{[}\citeproc{ref-okazawa_image_2015}{5},\citeproc{ref-okazawa_gradual_2017}{6}{]}.
Other analyses of between-region texture responses found that texture
stimuli with matched higher order image statistics produced higher
firing of V2 and V4 neurons compared to stimuli lacking higher order
image statistics
{[}\citeproc{ref-freeman_functional_2013}{2}--\citeproc{ref-lee_emergence_2025}{4}{]}.

\subsection{It is unknown whether trends in selectivity for image
statistics extend beyond
V4}\label{it-is-unknown-whether-trends-in-selectivity-for-image-statistics-extend-beyond-v4}

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../img/proposed_results.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-intro}(Top) A visual representation of two families
in some arbitrary 2D representation space. (Bottom) The distances
between families and between samples, with some arbitrary values for
each region.}

\end{figure}%

Lee et al.~(2024) recorded neural responses in V1, V2, V4, and IT,
spanning the entire range of the visual processing stream
{[}\citeproc{ref-lee_developmentally_2024}{3}{]}. Based on previous
results, I expect to find better between-group discrimination in later
areas and better within-group discrimination in earlier areas (see
Figure~\ref{fig-intro} for a schematic).

However, existing analyses tend to lack results for the entire visual
processing stream, so it is unclear if this trend will reliably extend
to IT (Jenelle mentions that previous results conflict over whether
texture coding exists in IT but I have not yet filled out this
background research). This gap is partially due to the practical
limitations of supporting a large population of animals, but there are
also methodological challenges with analyzing IT. Higher level areas
tend to produce sparse responses, so typical approaches in the
literature, such as size-matched population analyses or single-site
neuron metrics, may not produce reliable comparisons between IT and
earlier visual areas.

\subsection{Results for IT are dependent on the treatment of
sparsity}\label{results-for-it-are-dependent-on-the-treatment-of-sparsity}

I found that neural responses in IT are sparse in two different ways:
first, most IT neurons will not respond to any texture stimuli; and
second, of the IT neurons with any response to texture, only a small
number of unique stimuli will produce a response. For V1, V2, and V4,
recording sites that are unreliable (low correlation between responses
to the same stimuli) tend to also be unresponsive (low separability
between responses to different stimuli).

Depending on the reliable site criteria, unresponsive sites in IT tend
to achieve above-threshold reliability. Retaining reliable sites in IT
produces worse between-family separability in IT compared to V4, while
limiting analyses to responsive sites reverses this trend, producing
better between-family separability in IT compared to V4. The
within-family separability in IT remains consistently worse than V4.
This contrast suggests that IT destructively summarizes pixel-level
differences in images but efficiently encodes higher-level image
statistics.

\section{Tentative results and
commentary}\label{tentative-results-and-commentary}

\subsection{Characterizing texture
information}\label{characterizing-texture-information}

The degree of ``texture encoding information'' in IT is a bit difficult
to fully characterize with the given data since we lack the real texture
statistics of the stimuli. As a proxy, we can consider whether neural
responses contain encoding information about the texture family.

Mahalanobis distance can capture linear separability but is not robust
to variation in dimensionality. Generalization performance of
regularized regression models can be applied to between-family but not
within-family comparisons (due to lack of repetitions). Mutual
information measures are also dependent on dimensionality but may
produce a more general metric of dependence rather than assuming a
geometric structure to the separation.

\subsection{Site selection}\label{site-selection}

Doing site selection prior to analysis isn't necessarily bad.
Considering sites are typically not i.i.d., it might be useful to
discard sites based on a known criteria rather than use all the sites in
every region. Retaining all sites then correcting based on the different
dimensions could be misleading because of the non-i.i.d. nature of the
sites.

However, site selection does introduce biases. Previously, I computed
reliability with split-half correlation after aggregating over the
texture family then splitting by repetition number (even in one group,
odd in the other, for example). This strategy is consistent with
Gerick's previous work but also introduces the pattern of reliable IT
sites being unresponsive. Instead, one could do reliability measures
with split-half correlation aggregated over the sample image.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../img/split_corrs_ecdf.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-ecdf}Empirical cumulative distribution functions for
the split-half correlations by region. Data reflects split-half
correlations performed when grouped by sample images Around 75 percent
of IT sites fall below a split-half correlation of 0.25.}

\end{figure}%

In either case, the distributions of split-half correlations are
markedly different between regions (Figure~\ref{fig-ecdf}, though the
family aggregation results not shown). For example, the proportion of
sites with a split-half correlation below 0.25 is approximately 0.6 for
V1, 0.15 for V2, 0.4 for V4, and 0.75 for IT.

\subsection{Matched-reliability results between
families}\label{matched-reliability-results-between-families}

Jenelle suggests ``reliability-matching'' regions. For example, instead
of sub-sampling from all sites above a threshold, I would sub-sample
sites with split-half correlations in a similar range. This produces
Figure~\ref{fig-dM}, which shows a trend of increasing Mahalanobis
distances between families in progressive visual areas.

Previously, when doing reliable site selection with Gerick's
family-aggregated method, IT distance falls \emph{below} V4.

I basically agree with ``reliability-matching'' as a decent way to
address ``sparsity'' concerns. It discards the noisy sites and also
prevents the higher proportion of really reliable sites in V2 and V4
from basically dictating the distance/information metrics.

I did a hack job with Figure~\ref{fig-dM}, where I chose a range of
split-half correlation between 0.3 and 0.6 since the eCDFs in
Figure~\ref{fig-ecdf} look to have a similar slope in that range. There
are some modifications to just subsampling in a range. E.g., I could use
the empirical CDF to correct the probability of sampling any given site
such that the split-half correlations in the subsampled sites are
approximately uniform.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{../img/matched_reliability_dM_pf.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-dM}Mahalanobis distances between texture families in
subsampled sites with split-half correlations between 0.3 and 0.6. In
naturalistic textures (right), the distance between families increases
with progressive visual areas. The noisy textures, which lack coherent
texture statistics, serve as a sanity check (left).}

\end{figure}%

\subsection{Between-sample results}\label{between-sample-results}

I am still running the calculations for this, as I'm using Jenelle's
suggestion of randomized dichotomy calculations (essentially manifold
capacity). Within a family, samples are separated into two groups of 7
and the distance calculated between them (see previous reports for more
details).

Pairwise between-sample comparisons could also work. Previous results
suggest that these are pretty dependent on site reliability (though I
have not dived into this very deeply).

\subsection{Sparsity reports}\label{sparsity-reports}

Because of the non-i.i.d. site sampling, I would want to avoid claims
about the actual proportion of sparsity in IT. Other criteria, like the
number of images a site is responsive to given that a site is responsive
to at least one image, provide a more generic measure.

Sparsity is likely the cause of the difference in reliable/responsive
site calculations for Gerick's method of site selection (aggregated over
family) and my current method (aggregated over specific image).

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

Site selection based on matched reliability (split-half correlation in a
similar range when entries are aggregated over specific image and split
by repetition number) is intended to control for the sensitivity of
distance/information measures to dimensionality by discarding noisy
sites in each region. As currently implemented, I would not be able to
answer questions like ``what's the theoretical ceiling of texture
information between regions?'' since it discards both very reliable and
very unreliable sites. However, I suspect questions of this type are a
bit impossible to answer anyway considering the limited number of sites.

Texture families are a limited proxy of the target measure of image
statistic selectivity. I currently have no information about the
baseline similarity of families to each other. I am pretty sure (based
on EDA) that there are some families that produce reliable neural
responses in every region. It would be good to see if the results hold
in those families specifically.

Multi-unit activations may record from neighboring neurons, so any
discussion of sparsity should be attentive to the fact that specificity
is obscured (mostly relevant for IT).

Between-region and between-subject sparsity considerations prevents easy
selection of a holdout set.
