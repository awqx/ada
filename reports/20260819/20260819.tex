\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}\label{motivation}

Later stages of the visual cortex are thought to contain neurons
selective for progressively higher-level visual features.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The primary visual cortex (V1), for instance, contains neurons that
  selectively respond to small image features, e.g., oriented edges.
\item
  The feature tuning in the subsequent areas of the secondary visual
  cortex (V2) and visual area V4 is less well-characterized. Broadly, V2
  contains neurons selective for texture features and V4 contains
  neurons selective for geometric forms.
\item
  The inferior temporal gyrus (IT), which handles later visual
  processing, contains areas selective for object categories, such as
  the fusiform face area for facial recognition.
\item
  (??) The above is mostly from memory and may need to be fact-checked.
  E.g., the wording of ``feature tuning'' and ``selectively respond''
  may need to be more careful. ``Sensitive'' might be more appropriate.
\end{itemize}

One approach to characterize the relevant visual features for each layer
is through characterizing responses to visual textures with known image
statistics. Visual textures can be loosely described as approximately
homogeneous images with repeating but randomized elements, e.g., images
of sand, tree bark, granite, or honeycomb. Here, image statistics refer
to \(N\)th-order empirical densities of pixels. First order statistics
include, for example, luminance correlations at a given distance.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Why visual textures?} The theory of visual texture perception
  assumes that texture classes can be parameterized by a set of image
  statistics. If texture samples are within the same family, they should
  elicit comparable responses in the visual area where texture
  processing ``occurs'', even if the images are physically distinct.
\item
  Furthermore, the homogeneity in textures helps control for the
  different sizes of receptive fields in each visual area, since within
  some reasonable range, the statistics are consistent in differently
  sized slices of the textures.
\end{itemize}

Portilla and Simoncelli (2000)
{[}\citeproc{ref-portilla_parametric_2000}{1}{]} developed a parametric
texture model that, given a seed image of a visual texture, generates
new samples matched to the original's image statistics. The parametric
model can be adjusted to include higher-order statistics, which Portilla
and Simoncelli describe as V1-like. Matching V1-like correlations
produces more coherent \emph{naturalistic} structure. Example noisy (no
V1-like statistic matching) and naturalistic images can be seen in
Figure~\ref{fig-textures}. Freeman et al.~(2013) found that this
distinction may be relevant for functionally characterizing V2, finding
that V2 neurons tend to be more selective for naturalistic textures
{[}\citeproc{ref-freeman_functional_2013}{2}{]}.

My ADA will focus on further characterizing how image statistics may be
represented/transformed in V1, V2, V4, and IT, using data collected by
Lee et al.~(2024) {[}\citeproc{ref-lee_developmentally_2024}{3}{]}. V4
and IT are not usually recorded for visual texture stimuli, as higher
level areas tend to respond more sparsely to textures. Each family
contains samples that are matched to the same statistics, but each
sample is unique at a pixel level. Thus, if higher layers are selective
for certain higher level aggregated image statistics, we expect that
they will lose selectivity for the differences between samples.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  As a follow-up to Lee et al.~(2024), Gerrick Lee explored this
  question by comparing the distributions of pairwise between-family
  distances in each visual area, seen in (Figure~\ref{fig-areadne2024}).
  Consistent with their results finding better decoding of naturalistic
  vs.~noisy texture in V2 and V4, these areas show greater pairwise
  distances between naturalistic texture families compared to V1.
\item
  Distances in IT are smaller than V4, though I would like to explore
  whether this result is robust to corrections for the sparseness of
  responses to textures in IT.
\item
  ``Sparseness'' is loosely defined here, as there are different
  scenarios that could be described as ``sparse''. E.g.,
  ``site-sparseness'', where a few IT neurons reliably respond to
  texture families, or ``image-sparseness'', where for a given image,
  there are a few neurons whose response is reliable, but the responsive
  neurons for each image may differ.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data}\label{data}

I sourced data from Gerick Lee et al.~(2024), who collected longitudinal
and inter-region neural responses to visual texture stimuli (available
on \href{https://zenodo.org/records/10888270}{Zenodo})
{[}\citeproc{ref-lee_developmentally_2024}{3}{]}. Lee et al.~recorded
from 6 macaques with two arrays each, targeting V1, V2, V4, and IT. Lee
et al.~reported the multi-unit activations (MUAs); the spiking/raw
timing data are not available. Lee et al.~generated 15 samples of noisy
and naturalistic images from 35 families (\(15 \times 35 \times 2\)
unique images), where each family corresponded to a different original
seed image. The parameters fed into the PS model are lost, but the
images presented to the monkeys may be available.

\subsection{Expected results}\label{expected-results}

We expect to find better between-group discrimination in V2 and V4
better within-group discrimination in V1, i.e., more separation between
unique samples with the same visual statistics. That is, later visual
regions better encode relevant group-level visual statistics at the cost
of distinctively encoding individual instances of textures with the same
statistics (see Figure~\ref{fig-intro}). This effect is likely only
visible in population level metrics or decoding strategies considering
the relative size of the receptive fields. Previously, Ziemba et
al.~(2016) performed a similar analysis on recordings from V1 and V2
{[}\citeproc{ref-ziemba_selectivity_2016}{4}{]}. With nested ANOVA,
Ziemba et al.~found larger between-family variance in V1 but smaller
within-family contribution to variance. Ziemba et al.~(2016) also found
better performance of a Poisson decoder on classifying samples in V1
(and families in V2). Furthermore, for IT, it is possible that both
between-family and within-family distance decreases as IT may not be
sensitive to the image statistics that distinguish the textures, a
pattern found in Gerick Lee's previous analysis
(Figure~\ref{fig-areadne2024}).

\section{Analysis plan and methods}\label{analysis-plan-and-methods}

\subsection{Reliable site detection, onset time, and MUA
averaging}\label{reliable-site-detection-onset-time-and-mua-averaging}

To find visually responsive (reliable) sites, I followed Lee et al.'s
procedure. They averaged the site response within samples with the same
image statistics (so \(35 \times 2\) coherence-family combinations plus
one element for blank) then kept sites with a correlation of
\(\rho \geq 0.4\) between even and odd repetitions. They accounted for
different population sizes by repeatedly subsampling sites. Lee et
al.~defined the onset time for a site as the first time bin with a
\(|d'| \geq 0.4\) between non-blank and blank stimuli where the two
subsequent windows also produced \(|d'| \geq 0.4\) of the same sign (see
Equation~\ref{eq-dprime} for the definition of \(d'\), which is Cohon's
distance metric).

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-dprime}{
d' = \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{\sqrt{(\sigma^2_1 + \sigma^2_0)/2}}
}\end{equation}

The procedure for summarizing data can be outlined as:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Find a temporary population onset time, with \(d'\) calculated between
  blanks and stimuli. The search window is 0 to 200 ms after stimulus
  presentation. If no onset time is found, use 100 ms as a placeholder.
\item
  Determine the responsive sites with signals averaged over a 150 ms
  window starting at the temporary onset time.
\item
  Recalculate the population onset time based on the responsive sites.
\item
  For downstream analyses, sum responses across the 150 ms window after
  population onset time.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Distance metric for
separation}\label{distance-metric-for-separation}

To determine the between-family or between-sample separation within
regions, I will find the distribution of multivariate distance values
between pairwise comparisons. This approach will allow for visualization
of the median/mean pairwise distribution and the skew of the distances.

A multivariate distance value can be calculated with the typical
Mahalanobis distance formula, i.e., \[
d_M (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sqrt{(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})^\intercal \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})},
\] where \(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}\) correspond to the mean activity
vectors for each group, taken with respect to each site. The pooled
covariance matrix is the same as used in Hotelling's two-sample
statistic, derived as \[
\Sigma = 
\frac{(n_x - 1) \Sigma_x + (n_y - 1)\Sigma_y}{n_x + n_y - 2},
\] where \(\Sigma_x, \Sigma_y\) are the typical sample covariance
matrices for each distribution. \texttt{Rfast} contains functions
\texttt{Rfast::pooled.cov()} and \texttt{Rfast::spdinv()} to support
these calculations. For low trial numbers, the inverse covariance matrix
can be found with the regularized/shrinkage estimator from
\texttt{corpcor::invcov.shrink()}.

For between-family calculations, each family contains 90 trials (15
samples by 6 repetitions each). For between-sample distance
calculations, each sample only contains 6 repetitions. So, while
pairwise between-family calculations are straightforward, the
between-sample distances may need to be modified and explored more.

Jenelle proposed a randomized dichotomy procedure. Two groups of 7
sample IDs each (without replacement) will be selected. Because this is
not an even split of the available sample IDs, the procedure will
proceed as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Omit one of the 15 IDs (1, 2, \ldots, 15)
\item
  Take a random sample of the possible \(\binom{14}{7}\) combos of the
  remaining indices.
\item
  Find \(d_M\) between the two groups of seven samples (so 42 trials)
  each.
\item
  Repeat the above for each of the 15 IDs.
\end{enumerate}

Alternatively, we could still perform pairwise between-sample
calculations (with the regularized/shrinkage estimator) and discuss
trends visible within families.

\subsection{Sparseness
characterization}\label{sparseness-characterization}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  (??) I'm returning to this more exploratory question since I glossed
  over it in the reliable site detection method applied previously.
\end{itemize}

Very few investigators record visual texture responses from IT due to
the lack of a strong, coherent response. \emph{A priori}, we should
expect a sparse response in IT. There are a few ways that sparseness may
appear in IT:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  There are a few IT neurons whose responses to the texture families
  tend to be strong and reliable.
\item
  For each texture family, there are a few neurons whose response is
  reliable, but the responsive neurons for each family may differ.
\item
  For some texture families, there are a few neurons whose response is
  reliable.
\item
  Same as (3), but for each specific image (sample ID).
\item
  Same as (3), but for each specific image.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Because reliable sites are identified by correlation to the same
  stimuli, it is possible to consistently miss patterns of sparsity
  outside of (1) in my previously described procedure.
\item
  Since scenario (5) is most general, we want to focus on approaches to
  characterize the neural response that would work in this situation.
\item
  The most basic analysis is to perform a two sample Welch's t-test for
  each neuron's response for a specific image compared to the response
  to blank images.
\item
  Visualizing the main components of the \emph{t}-test (the difference
  in means and the standard deviations) would also be useful.
\end{itemize}

The statistic \emph{t} is found as \[
t = \frac
{ \overline{X}_1 - \overline{X}_2 }
{ \sqrt{s^2_{\overline{X}_1} + s^2_{\overline{X}_2}} }, 
\quad 
s_{\overline{X}_i} = \frac{s_i}{\sqrt{n_i}}
\] where \(\overline{X}_i\) is the sample mean, and
\(s_{\overline{X}_i}\) is the standard error, \(s_i\) is the sample
standard deviation, and \(n_i\) is the sample size. The
\emph{t}-distribution's degrees of freedom can be approximated as \[
\nu \approx \frac
{ \left( s^2_{\overline{X}_1} + s^2_{\overline{X}_2} \right)^2 }
{ \frac{s^4_{\overline{X}_1}}{n_1 - 1} + \frac{s^4_{\overline{X}_2}}{n_2 - 1} }.
\] For simplicity, this will be implemented by \texttt{t.test()}.

\section{Data}\label{sec-data}

Monkeys were presented with textures generated from the PS parametric
texture model {[}\citeproc{ref-portilla_parametric_2000}{1}{]}, with
unique images created from 2 levels of coherence (noisy/naturalistic),
35 families (seed images), and 15 randomly generated samples from each
family. This produces 1050 unique images (\(35 \times 2 \times 15\)).
There are six repetitions of each image per session.

Lee et al.~recorded from 6 monkeys, each implanted with two 96-site Utah
arrays. Within a session, there were 6 repetitions of each of the
\(35 \times 15 \times 2 = 1050\) unique images, presented in blocks of 8
images (100 ms on, 100 ms off). The provided files aggregate recordings
by region, with the number of sites per region in Table~\ref{tbl-sites}.
For each of V1, V2, V4, and IT, there is at least one 96-electrode Utah
array. The published per-session data reflects simultaneously recorded
sites.

On M1, one array sat on the border between V1 and V2. For M3, the
authors could not reliably determine the location and labeled the region
as the foveal confluence (FC) of V1, V2 and V4, which I removed from my
analyses. Lee et al.~implanted IT arrays twice in M6, with the recording
at 221 weeks taken from the second implantation.

\subsection{Between sample
comparisons}\label{between-sample-comparisons}

With only six samples, the inverse covariance matrix needs to be found
with a shrinkage estimator, such as the one provided by
\texttt{corpcor::invcov.shrink()}, which implements the Ledoit-Wolf
shrinkage estimator. When the distances are aggregated over families,
the pairwise distances tend to be largest for V2, a trend that seems to
be consistent when the values are separated by families
(Figure~\ref{fig-pairwise_dM}). This is somewhat inconsistent with
Ziemba et al.'s results, which found that samples were better separated
in V1.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{20260819_files/figure-pdf/fig-pairwise_dM-1.pdf}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-pairwise_dM}Histogram of Mahalanobis distances for
each pairwise sample comparison, either aggregated across all families
(top) or separated by family (bottom) and with fill color based on

must be found with the shrinkage estimator.}

The randomized dichotomy results, in order of increasing median
distance, tends to follow V1 \textless{} IT \textless{} V4 \textless{}
V2, which is also not consistent with the previous results in Ziemba et
al.~(2016). Results will be reported with the shrinkage estimator. The
number of sites used does not change the overall pattern of the
distances, but more sites tends to increase the range of calculated
distance values (Figure~\ref{fig-dM_infam}). This does not change with
the ordinary (non-shrinkage) estimator (not shown). I have not yet
analyzed the difference in means and \(\Sigma^{-1}\) for the randomized

\section{Next steps}\label{next-steps}

\subsection{Immediate additional
analyses}\label{immediate-additional-analyses}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  I want to check if z-scoring MUAs affects the trends seen in the
  between-family and between-sample comparisons.
\item
  Mostly, I do not have a good intuition for how potential sparsity in
  V4 and IT may affect the comparisons, especially if sparsity is not
  universal.
\item
  I am not sure z-scoring will help with this exact question, though
  inconsistency between the current analyses and the z-scored analyses
  will probably indicate some interesting feature of the covariance
  between sites.
\item
  The between-sample (within-family) results being inconsistent with
  Ziemba et al.~needs to be explained. Ziemba et al.~drew their
  conclusions with multi-level ANOVA (decomposing variance into a
  between-family, between-sample, and between-repetition component) and
  Poisson regression. Possibly, if there is overall higher variance in
  V2 firing, the scaling of the \(d_M\) comparisons may not
  ``normalized'' compared to ANOVA. However, it is unclear to me why
  \(d_M\) would differ from Poisson regression results.
\item
  Furthermore, it might be prudent to consider holding out arrays from
  V4 and IT. It is likely not possible for V1 and V2, though future
  analyses may be performed on the Ziemba et al.~data.
\end{itemize}
