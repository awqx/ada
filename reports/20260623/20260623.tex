\section{Overview}\label{overview}

When modeling brain activations with artificial neural networks (ANNs),
previous work found more shared information between early ANN layers and
early visual areas and, accordingly, late ANN layers and late visual
areas
{[}\citeproc{ref-schrimpf_integrative_2020}{1}--\citeproc{ref-nonaka_brain_2021}{3}{]}.
These analyses often report the quality of fit of linear models
predicting brain activity from ANN activations
{[}\citeproc{ref-schrimpf_brain-score_2020}{4}{]}. However, as discussed
in Feather and Canatar (2023), early and late region/layer data are
often not sourced from the same experiment. Thus, these results might be
confounded with task differences
{[}\citeproc{ref-canatar_spectral_2023}{5}{]}.

In my ADA, I am analyzing dataset provided by Lee et al.~(2024)
containing longitudinal and inter-region neural responses to visual
texture stimuli, available on
\href{https://zenodo.org/records/10888270}{Zenodo}
{[}\citeproc{ref-lee_developmentally_2024}{6}{]}. Lee et al.~recorded
from 6 macaques with two arrays each, targeting V1, V2, V4, and IT (with
the recorded region differing by monkey). All recordings were taken from
the same set of texture stimuli.

First, I want to characterize the differences in neural responses for
each region and establish differences in encoding of the stimuli. Lee et
al.~have already performed linear discriminant analysis between
noisy/naturalistic textures, and concluded that neural discriminability
was highest in V4, lower in V2 and IT, and lowest in V1. For the ADA, I
want to characterize encoding differences between texture families.
Possible approaches include:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Multiclass logistic regression (plus some other more detailed
  interpretation than the inference performance)
\item
  Some non-parametric clustering strategy
\item
  Some distance metric then geometric analysis of the distance matrix
\end{itemize}

Then, to pursue the question of whether early/late model alignment,
possible approaches include:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Fiting linear models to predict the activity in one region from the
  activity of another region
\item
  Using V1-like statistics from the provided textures or produce
  predictors from an established model of V1 firing
\item
  Training an ANN on the noisy/naturalistic or family classification
  task then comparing the early/late layer alignment
\end{itemize}

\section{Data description}\label{data-description}

Prior to publishing the data used here, this research group claimed that
V2 played a functional role in discriminating noisy vs.~naturalistic
textures {[}\citeproc{ref-freeman_functional_2013}{7}{]}. This follow-up
examined how neural and behavioral sensitivity changes over development.

Lee et al.~generated stimuli from the Portilla and Simoncelli (PS)
parametric texture model
{[}\citeproc{ref-portilla_parametric_2000}{8}{]}, producing 15 samples
from 35 families at two levels of coherence (noisy vs.~naturalistic).
They provided four repetitions of each unique image in each session.
They implanted two 96-electrode Utah arrays in 6 monkeys.

\subsection{Texture stimuli}\label{texture-stimuli}

Example textures generated from the Portilla and Simoncelli (2001) are
in Figure~\ref{fig-textures}. The PS parametric texture model is a
multilevel generative model that produces sample images by matching
visual statistics to an original seed image. The lowest level of the
model matches the local statistic of the original image, e.g., the
correlation in luminance between adjacent pixels, the average luminance
in a small area, or the total variation in luminance. Subsequent layers
control higher level correlations, e.g., correlations between
neighboring patterns of pixels.

Portilla and Simoncelli describe the higher levels as ``V1-like'' or
analogous to the receptive fields of the primary visual cortex. Matching
V1-like correlations produces more coherent \emph{naturalistic}
structure. Example noisy and naturalistic images can be seen in
Figure~\ref{fig-textures} (left). Though the degree of naturalistic
structure varied in the behavioral task, the passive fixation recordings
only contained noisy/zero strength textures or full strength
naturalistic textures. Lee et al.~generated 15 samples of noisy and
naturalistic images from 35 families (\(15 \times 35 \times 2\) unique
images), where each family corresponded to a different original seed
image.

\subsection{Passive recording setup}\label{passive-recording-setup}

At least two recording sessions were conducted for each monkey: once at
a juvenile stage (around 30 weeks) and once at an adult stage (around 56
weeks). Within sessions, images were shown in blocks of 8, with 100 ms
of the image on screen followed by 100 ms of blank display. Each of the
1050 unique images (35 families, 15 samples, 2 levels of structure) were
presented four times within a session. Lee et al.~also included 100
uniquely labeled blank images, also repeated four times. This gives a
total of \(4 \times (35 \times 15 \times 2 + 100) = 4600\) observations
per session.

\subsection{Recording details}\label{recording-details}

Lee et al.~recorded from 6 out of 8 monkeys, with experimental details
recopied in Appendix Table~\ref{tbl-lee_exp}. The behavioral task is
probably not relevant for the ADA, but I included a brief overview in
the Appendix Section~\ref{sec-behavior}. The number of visually
responsive sites for each area and session can be seen in
Table~\ref{tbl-sites} in Section~\ref{sec-processing}.

The authors implanted a pair of 96 electrode Utah arrays per monkey
(i.e., a total of 192 channels per subject). Regions were determined
with anatomical landmarks, physiological features (e.g., response
latency), and receptive field characteristics. One array implanted on M1
sat on the border between V1 and V2. For M3, the authors could not
reliably determine the location and labeled the region as FC (the
\emph{foveal confluence} of V1, V2 and V4), based on terminology from
Brewer et al. {[}\citeproc{ref-brewer_visual_2002}{9}{]}. I removed FC
data from analysis.

Lee et al.~implanted arrays twice in M6, with the recording at 221 weeks
taken from the second implantation. Two monkeys (M4 and M5) only
underwent behavioral experiments. One monkey (M8) only underwent passive
fixation recording. Three monkeys (M1, M2, and M3) performed the
behavioral task prior to passive fixation experiments. Two monkeys (M6
and M7) provided passive fixation recordings prior to undergoing
behavioral tests.

The provided \href{https://zenodo.org/records/10888270}{Zenodo} data
files do not contain the original spike timings. Multiunit threshold
crossings, or multiunit activations (MUAs), were created from binning
spikes into 10 ms windows. Single neuron spikes seem more common in
dimensionality analyses
{[}\citeproc{ref-pospisil_revisiting_2025}{10},\citeproc{ref-cowley_stimulus-driven_2016}{11}{]},
but other work covers functional dimensionality estimation on fMRI data
{[}\citeproc{ref-ahlheim_estimating_2018}{12}{]}, so this aggregation is
probably fine for the purpose of the ADA.

\section{Processing multiunit activations}\label{sec-processing}

To find visually responsive (reliable) sites, Lee et al.~averaged the
site response separately for even and odd repetitions of each unique
image stimulus (i.e., two groups of two repetitions each) then kept
sites with a Pearson correlation of \(\rho \geq 0.4\). Lee et
al.~defined the onset time for a site as the first time bin with a
\(|d'| \geq 0.4\) between non-blank and blank stimuli where the two
subsequent windows also produced \(|d'| \geq 0.4\) of the same sign (see
Equation~\ref{eq-dprime} for the definition of \(d'\)). They defined the
population onset time as the median across visually responsive sites.

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-dprime}{
d' = \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{\sqrt{(\sigma^2_1 + \sigma^2_0)/2}}
}\end{equation}

The procedure for summarizing data can be outlined as:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Find a temporary population onset time, with \(d'\) calculated between
  blanks and stimuli. The search window is 0 to 200 ms after stimulus
  presentation. If no onset time is found, use 100 ms as a placeholder.
\item
  Determine the responsive sites with signals averaged over a 150 ms
  window starting at the temporary onset time.
\item
  Recalculate the population onset time based on the responsive sites.
\item
  For downstream analyses, sum responses across the 150 ms window after
  population onset time.
\end{enumerate}

I found fewer reliable recording sites than Lee et al., seen in
Table~\ref{tbl-sites} (see Section~\ref{sec-concerns} for discussion).
Table~\ref{tbl-onsets} contain my calculated onset times and the
proportion of reliable sites where onset time could be found. As a
sanity check, onset times increase from V1 to IT. The proportion of
reliable sites with derivable onset time decreases along region
hierarchy.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Concerns and assumptions}\label{sec-concerns}

\textbf{Stimulus presentation}. I assumed that stimuli were presented at
index 50 in each MATLAB file based on the averaged responses.

\textbf{Responsive site calculations}. In split-half correlation
calculations, I treated the arbitrarily labeled 100 blank images (4
repetitions each) as unique images like the texture images. However, Lee
et al.~may have omitted the blanks or summarized them, explaining the
differences in Table~\ref{tbl-sites}.

\textbf{Aggregating across subjects}. The number of recording sites is
pretty low, so I aggregated across subjects. For IT, unless the
responsive site discrepancy is addressed, I might also need to aggregate
across ages.

\subsection{Recording and behavioral task details}\label{sec-behavior}

Lee et al.~measured behavioral sensitivities with a four-choice oddity
task. Monkeys were shown 3 noisy distractors (all unique) and 1
naturalistic target, with all four images produced from the same family.
To calculate sensitivity curves, Lee et al.~varied the strength of the
naturalistic structure/V1-like statistic matching. Behavioral
sensitivity to naturalistic structure improved with age, though neural
sensitivity remained consistent.
