\section{Procedures}\label{procedures}

\subsection{Mean-centering neurons}\label{mean-centering-neurons}

The column means (corresponding to the mean response of a neuron
averaged across all stimuli) were subtracted from each column. To
prevent leakage, centering was performed separately for each split.

\subsection{Split half PCA}\label{split-half-pca}

Recordings were split into odd (training) and even (testing) repetitions
of each unique image stimulus. Repetitions of the same stimulus were
averaged. PCA was performed on the training set with \texttt{prcomp()}.
The test set was then projected onto the PCs. The test signal was then
reconstructed with \(1, 2, \dots, n\) PCs, where \(n\) was the number of
responsive sites for a region/age.

I found the proportion of variance explained for the training set with
cumulative sums of the PCA eigenvalues, scaled by the overall sum. For
the test set, I reported the proportion of variance explained with a
standard \(R^2\) calculation.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Different stimuli subsets and included
channels}\label{different-stimuli-subsets-and-included-channels}

The split half PCA procedure follows the same logic as split half
correlation. However, unlike correlation, blank stimuli were removed.
For this analysis, I ran the split half PCA procedure with 1) all
non-blank stimuli, 2) noisy stimuli, and 3) naturalistic stimuli.

I also compared performance on only responsive sites vs.~all available
sites. For figures with only responsive sites, the number of included
PCs has been scaled to the proportion of total PCs. For figures with all
available sites, I did not alter the scale of the number of PCs, but I
did truncate graphs to the first 96 PCs, corresponding to the number of
channels on a single array.

The number of PCs to explain 90\% of maximum test set R-squared for
non-blank stimuli, noisy stimuli, and naturalistic stimuli can be found
in Table~\ref{tbl-pca} (in analyses with only responsive sites).The
maximum test set R-squared can be found in Table~\ref{tbl-pca_rsq}.

\subsection{R-squared mostly consistent with Lee et al.'s
results}\label{r-squared-mostly-consistent-with-lee-et-al.s-results}

The R-squared trends in Table~\ref{tbl-pca} seem consistent with
\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2024.114534}{Lee et al.} finding
that V4 and V2 have the best discriminability between noisy and
naturalistic stimuli in LDA. That is, the higher reconstructed
R-squareds reflect more usable information in those recordings.

\subsection{IT flatter, V4 peakier}\label{it-flatter-v4-peakier}

IT appears to have a flatter eigenspectrum in @fig\_pca, though this
might be an artifact of the low number of responsive sites/PCs. The
analysis with all available plots might be helpful for region-age
combinations with a small number of responsive sites. These plots show a
clear plateau where the training PCs only capture noise in the test set.
Instead of the 90\% variance explained threshold, a better metric may be
the location of the elbow where the plateau begins (not yet calculated).

The eigenspectrum of V4 seems consistently ``peakier'', with more weight
on earlier eigenvalues. There appears to be a trend where V1 is
flattest, V4 is peakiest, and V2 is in-between. Next steps can explore
this more.

\subsection{Worse reconstruction of noisy
stimuli}\label{worse-reconstruction-of-noisy-stimuli}

In Figure~\ref{fig-pca}, on the test set, naturalistic-only stimuli to
have better reconstruction than non-blank, which in turn is better than
noisy-only stimuli. (I double-checked that the centering of columns
occurred \emph{after} subsetting the stimuli type). This trend probably
implies that the noisy texture stimuli produced more noise in the signal
response.

Generally, fewer PCs are needed to explain 90\% of the variance of the
test set when the stimuli only include the noisy or naturalistic
stimuli. Noisy-only stimuli tend to require fewer PCs than naturalistic
stimuli.

\subsection{Averages in early and late windows after
onset}\label{averages-in-early-and-late-windows-after-onset}

Out of curiosity, I also created Figure~\ref{fig-pca_stages}, which
contains the plots of explained variance when the signal average is
taken for 50 ms after onset (``early'') or 100-150 ms after onset
(``late''). Since averages are taken after onset time calculation, the
``early'' and ``late'' windows were different for each region
(corresponding to rows in Figure~\ref{fig-pca_stages}). I did not spot
any consistent or notable differences in the curves for the training
set, so they are omitted for clarity.

The order of reconstruction performance, from worst to best, tends to go
from early to late to full window. Eigenspectra tend to be consistent
with stimulus set, regardless of the window where averages are taken.

I don't have a confident explanation as to why the slopes of the
eigenspectra after the elbow are different for the stimulus types or
even a guess as to whether it's that important. It might reflect the
split-half correlation/self-consistency of neurons, though I would need
to check this against the learned PCs.

\section{Next steps}\label{next-steps}

When noisy and naturalistic stimuli were grouped together, the
eigenspectrum did not obviously differ from the eigenspectra derived
from the noisy-only and naturalistic-only stimuli. This may indicate
that the most influential eigenvalues/eigenvectors for all non-blank
stimuli did not correspond to the noisy/naturalistic distinction. So, a
reasonable next step would be to investigate predictiveness of the
noisy-naturalistic distinction as a function of the number of included
eigenvalues. Relatedly, checking predictiveness for other features, like
the family, block order, etc. might be interesting.

Furthermore, I might want to backtrack on the responsive sites to do
some additional exploratory analyses. I am somewhat interested in
whether responsive sites are all up- or downregulated.

One consistent pattern throughout analyses is that V4 tends to look more
``peaky''/have more influential early eigenvalues. From Lee et al.'s
original publication, we know V4 is the most predictive of the
noisy-naturalistic distinction. There might be something there, i.e.,
questions like:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Do the initial principal components of V4 contain more information
  about the noisy-naturalistic distinction?
\item
  Are the PCs of V4 linearly related to the PCs of V2? or IT?
\item
  Does V2 lack predictiveness of the noisy-naturalistic distinction or
  is it nonlinearly decodable?
\end{itemize}
